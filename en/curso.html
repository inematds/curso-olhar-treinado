Choose references with a question

You can select three references to study one decision.

A collection of beautiful images can grow without teaching you a decision. A question helps you select references you can use.

In 1 minute

  1. Question: where does yellow draw attention to the subject?
  2. Select: three references that help answer the question
  3. There is no universal beauty that replaces the goal.

1Taste as a set of decisions

Visual taste can be described through choices you recognize and repeat: color, light, composition, texture, and rhythm.

In this course, we practice observing and explaining these choices without defining a universal standard of beauty.

Saying “I like it” helps you choose, but it does not explain how to make another image.

A specific observation lets you turn a preference into a work decision.

André, a photographer, applies this choice to material for the fictional Bairro Vivo fair: Select three images to observe how soft light shows fabrics and people at a fair.

2Curation: choose with a question

Curation means selecting references for a purpose.

A board for studying side lighting is different from a collection of beautiful images.

The question guides what goes in and what stays out.

A huge, unorganized archive makes patterns harder to notice.

A few well-annotated examples can be more useful for the exercise.

Separate what you see from what you interpret

You can record observations, interpretations, and a pattern.

A feeling is not the same as visible evidence. Separating the two keeps you from turning personal taste into certainty about an audience.

In 1 minute

  1. Observation: soft light and empty chairs
  2. Interpretation: it may suggest a pause
  3. A suggested feeling is not a proven audience reaction.

1Observation and interpretation

Observation describes what is visible: a light background, an isolated object, a short shadow.

Interpretation suggests a meaning: calm, familiarity, or precision.

Both are useful, but they are not the same thing.

Separating the two keeps impressions from being presented as facts.

It also makes it possible to discuss why two people read the same image differently.

André, a photographer, applies this choice to material for the fictional Bairro Vivo fair: The scene has soft light and few objects. This may suggest calm for this project, but it is still an interpretation.

2Find patterns without erasing differences

A pattern is something that recurs across references: a limited palette, side lighting, close-up shots, or matte materials.

It can guide a shared visual language without requiring identical images.

Noting only the subject—all of them have posters—is not enough to understand style.

You need to notice how each subject was represented.

Broad adjectives can point in different directions. Translating the intent into light, color, and placement lets you make and check a study.

In 1 minute

  1. Intent: welcome visitors

1Turn adjectives into instructions

Visual vocabulary turns broad expressions into observable choices.

Welcoming might mean soft window light, earthy tones, and a few everyday objects. That is one possible direction, not a universal equivalent.

A specific instruction can be produced and evaluated.

If you ask for “elegant” without defining visual clues, it is hard to tell what needs to be fixed.

A visual study is a small practice focused on one technique.

It could mean recreating a lighting approach with a different object or rearranging a composition while keeping the content.

Repeating a technique with intention helps you recognize its effects.

A routine should lead to observation and application, not just more saved images.

Compare minimalist and editorial directions

You can compare two directions by their use of space and hierarchy.

A visual choice is more useful when you can explain what you observed. In this lesson, turn your first impression into a decision you can check.

In 1 minute

  1. Minimalist: few elements and ample space
  2. Editorial: composition and scale guide the reading
  3. The families are a study map, not rigid boxes.

1Aesthetic is a combination of signs

Aesthetic is a visual language formed by choices in color, light, composition, texture, and shape.

The seven families in this module are a study map: cinematic, minimalist, editorial, dreamlike, retro, raw, and futuristic.

The names help us talk, but they do not replace description.

Two images called cinematic may use very different approaches.

André, a photographer, applies this choice to material for the fictional Bairro Vivo fair: A minimalist direction reduces elements. An editorial direction creates a striking entrance. Choose based on what the message needs to communicate.

2Minimalist and editorial

A minimalist direction reduces elements and highlights spatial relationships.

An editorial direction may use striking composition, scale contrast, styling, and typography.

Neither one requires a single palette.

Comparing them shows that clarity and impact can come from different approaches.

The goal guides how many elements to use and what role they play.

Recognize signs of four aesthetics

You can connect four studies to observable clues.

A visual choice is more useful when you can explain what you observed. In this lesson, turn your first impression into a decision you can check.

In 1 minute

  1. Cinematic: depth and a situation that suggests a story
  2. Dreamlike: dream relationships; retro: signs of another era
  3. The same work can combine cues from several families.

1Cinematic and dreamlike

Cinematic visual language can use depth, contrast, and a situation that suggests a story.

Dreamlike visual language can explore soft transitions, gentle light, and less literal relationships between elements.

Both can create atmosphere, but they may not suit showing product details.

Balance mood with readability.

André, a photographer, applies this choice to material for the fictional Bairro Vivo fair: Describe depth, contrast, and situation. For another direction, choose print texture, direct light, or dreamlike relationships.

2Retro and raw

Retro refers to visual cues from other eras through color, composition, typography, or finish.

Raw visual language can use strong contrast, pronounced textures, and less polished framing.

These are expressive choices, not automatic flaws.

Naming the specific cue prevents every grain from becoming vintage or every shadow from feeling intense.

Describe the reference precisely.

1Futurism and its substyles

Futuristic visual language can combine geometry, reflective surfaces, luminous contrast, or technical organization.

A substyle is a more specific direction within a broad visual repertoire, such as a restrained technological look or a saturated night city.

The label “future” does not define the intent.

A campaign about precision may need space and order; one about fiction may explore density and strangeness.

André, a photographer, applies this choice to material for the fictional Bairro Vivo fair: Use a minimalist structure with one subtle luminous accent. The fair remains the subject, not the effect.

2Combine styles with a hierarchy

A hybrid direction combines elements from different visual repertoires.

It is easier to read when one system organizes the composition and another adds a language detail.

Mixing five labels in a prompt can create conflict.

Choose one dominant style and one secondary element so you can observe the effect of the combination.

An irregular surface can support a handmade idea; precise geometry can support a technical presentation.

These are contextual hypotheses.

A piece can combine cues that point in different directions.
